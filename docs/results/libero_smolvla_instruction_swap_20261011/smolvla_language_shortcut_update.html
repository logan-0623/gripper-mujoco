Research update · Preliminary findings13 October 2026

Language changes behavior.
Does it ground the task?

In one SmolVLA training run, swapped instructions produced approach paths closer to their source-task demonstrations. The next question is whether language selects a familiar action pattern or identifies the correct target in the current scene.

Zihong (Logan) Luo · SmolVLA · LIBERO Spatial, tasks 0–3

Same initial scene. Different instruction.

At each checkpoint, compare correct, empty, random-text, and other-task instructions using matched initial states and policy noise seeds. Other-task instructions rotate among the four tasks. Policy weights stay fixed during evaluation; subsequent observations evolve with the robot’s actions.

  • 5 checkpoints · 5k–25k steps
  • 40 initial states per task
  • 3,200 rollouts in the main comparison
  • 1 training seed
01

Two observations

Working interpretation: language may cue familiar action patterns

The results establish sensitivity to instruction input in this setting. The trajectory evidence is consistent with a language-to-trajectory shortcut: an instruction may select behavior associated with its training task.

The unresolved test is grounding. These swaps do not provide a clear alternative target that uniquely satisfies the new instruction in the current scene. Empty and random inputs are also outside the training instruction distribution. Correct execution under meaningful alternative instructions remains to be tested.

Measurement details and scope

The trace follow-up contains 480 swapped-instruction rollouts plus 160 correct-instruction calibration rollouts. These are separate from the 3,200-rollout main comparison. All checkpoints come from one training run, so the 480 trajectories are not independent training replications.

DTW compares end-effector approach paths through one step after the first close command, or through the lowest end-effector position if no close command occurs. Paths are resampled to 25 points. The reported distance is the accumulated DTW cost divided by the sum of the two sequence lengths.

A separate endpoint classification used a 5 cm preregistered radius, which failed calibration (102/136 successful correct-instruction rollouts within radius). A 6.6 cm radius, chosen from the correct-instruction calibration data after the swapped results were available, covered 123/136. Endpoint classifications depend on this radius and are not the basis of the headline claim. The 480/480 path-similarity result is independent of that radius.

02

How this fits existing work

Language sensitivity and reliable grounding are different properties. These preliminary results help distinguish how a policy responds to language.

Language neglect
LIBERO-PRO and LIBERO-Plus report settings where corrupted or removed language has little effect. The current SmolVLA run shows a different sensitivity pattern; the models and protocols are not matched.
Alternative targets
LIBERO-CF and LangGap test alternative tasks in the same scene. These designs help evaluate whether changed instructions lead to the correct new target. LIBERO-CF also reports model-dependent performance drops after removing language.
Wording sensitivity
LIBERO-Para reports failures under meaning-preserving rewording, particularly changes to object references. This illustrates why a strong response to text can coexist with unreliable grounding.
03

Next experiments

  1. Keep meaning, change wording. Test paraphrases of the original instruction to measure dependence on familiar phrasing.
  2. Change the goal within the same scene. Use an unambiguous, reachable alternative target, placing it where grounding and a familiar source-task trajectory predict different actions. Score the new target and task.
  3. Check transfer to π₀. Repeat matched controls to test whether the observed pattern extends beyond this SmolVLA training run.
